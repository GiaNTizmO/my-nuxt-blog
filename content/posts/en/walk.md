## When the next attempt looks like the last one

There comes a point when reading the code stops adding information. Your eyes run over familiar lines, your hands tweak one small thing after another, but the hypothesis stays the same.

At that point a break can be more useful than another browser tab. Not because every problem is solved by a walk, but because a change of context helps you notice that you are repeating yourself.

## Leave yourself a way back in

Before the break, write down three things:

1. What behaviour you expected.
2. What you actually observe.
3. Which next experiment could tell your two current hypotheses apart.

That way, getting back to the task starts with a question instead of trying to reconstruct your whole train of thought.

## Don't turn rest into a tool

A walk does not have to be productive. Sometimes it is just a walk: a street, light, wind, people busy with completely different things.

Not every free minute has to improve your output. Time outside the project is valuable even without a lucky commit afterwards.

> Code is an important part of the day. But the day is usually bigger than the code.

## Come back with one question

After the break, reread your short note and run one experiment. Sometimes a new idea appears, sometimes not. Either way, it becomes clearer which step you are taking and why.
